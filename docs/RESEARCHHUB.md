## Knots of Existence: what does a foam roller release?

A call for letters of intent

### Purpose

The aim is to find out exactly what one particular kind of knot is: the kind that lets go within seconds under a foam
roller or a bodyworker's hand, with a release the person feels at once and, often, a sudden rush of feeling: relief or
well-being, at times tears or euphoria. That felt release is what marks these knots out. Bodyworkers, people who
foam-roll and some contemplative practitioners all describe them. No mechanism for them is considered established and,
as far as is known, nothing has been recorded at the moment one lets go. The question is about the knot itself, not
the general effects of foam rolling.

This call invites short letters of intent for studies that might help. A useful result may be positive, negative or
inconclusive. Fourteen ideas for studies are set out on the project's Research page, each with what it might show and
what it likely needs: https://ejhong.github.io/knots/research/

Twelve are on knots. Two are on a second, smaller question, what runaway release is: a rarer and dangerous process,
described near the end of this call.

### The knots meant here

A knot is subjectively felt as a bump, from very small to about the size of a penny; it seldom hurts unless pressed
hard, as under a foam roller, so tenderness does not define it. Its release does. A knot lets go within seconds, most
familiarly under a roller or a bodyworker's hand for example, though possibly due primarily to the breath, and the
person feels it go, often with a tingle over the spot. A rush of feeling is often reported to follow at once: relief
or well-being, at times tears or euphoria, and most of all when a whole cluster lets go together. These are the knots
meant here.

A knot does not always stand out. An area with many knots seems to be less flexible, and is felt more as a dull block.
A trained breath may be needed for the person to feel its knots as distinct bumps, and to release them; another's hand
might possibly feel them too. As the knots let go, the block seems to ease. So an examiner's hand on a body at rest might
find no bumps where there are many knots.

### One hypothesis, and six others

A position paper, *The perforator hypothesis: knots as small arteries held shut where they pierce the fascia* (on the
project's site: https://ejhong.github.io/knots/research/perforator-hypothesis/), argues from established physiology
that a knot is a cutaneous perforator, a
small artery held shut by its own wall where it pierces the fascia. The idea grew from vasocomputation, the proposal
that held predictions are held vascular tension (Johnson 2023), whose first step it keeps, a held contraction as a
stored state; and from two reports of runaway release, described below. It is untested. The paper sets out six
alternatives in their
strongest forms (smooth muscle in the latch state, a myofascial trigger point, densified hyaluronan, a sensitised nerve,
motor units that latch on, and perception) and the measurements that would refute it. The project's site sets out all
seven, each at its strongest: https://ejhong.github.io/knots/hypotheses/ (code and data:
https://github.com/ejhong/knots). Studies that count against the hypothesis are as welcome as studies that support it.

The paper was written by AI under its author's direction and has not been peer-reviewed, and its author will not take it
to a journal. If, having weighed the alternatives, you think the perforator hypothesis is the best fit, take it up: test
it, rework it, publish it (the paper is CC BY 4.0). A letter can propose that write-up, and it can be funded. If you think
a rival theory fits better, the models, data and code are open to you too.

### You are the experts

The studies on the Research page are ideas from a non-expert working with AI, meant to show the kind of thing that
might help. They were assessed only quickly, and some may prove not useful, or not worth doing now. You know the
methods, the pitfalls and what is feasible: please make your own assessment of what would really be useful. Three
judgements would help more than any one idea there:

- **The lowest-hanging fruit.** Which could be done soon and cheaply, and still tell something?
- **What to address first.** The list starts with finding knots, since much of the rest builds on it. Is that where to
  begin, and what should be taken up now?
- **What you could do.** Which could you take on, with the skills, instruments, laboratory, patients or data you have,
  alone or with others?

Or propose something better: the study you think would actually decide the question matters more than any listed.

One likely place to start, though you may judge otherwise, is with what people report. Two studies begin there, with
surveys and interviews that need no instruments and could start now: what people who foam-roll feel (study 1), asked
of them and of bodyworkers, and a description of runaway release (study 13), from people who know it at first hand. One
team might do both. Neither need be large: their use is to tell the studies that measure whom to recruit, and where
and when to look.

### What is asked: a letter of intent

This call depends on your expertise, and a short letter is enough to give it: a page or less, which should not take
long to write.

- **Your reading of the list.** Which studies are the lowest-hanging fruit, and which should be addressed first,
  including ones you would not take on yourself.
- **What you would do.** The question, what you would measure, in whom and with what, and what the result might show.
  If it studies knots, say how you would know they are the ones meant here.
- **Who would do it.** The people and their relevant experience, the instruments, laboratory, participants or data
  already to hand, and any competing interests.
- **Time and cost.** A rough timeline and a rough total cost. If a smaller first step would still tell something, say
  what it is and what it would cost.

One study, or part of one, is enough. A reading of the list helps even without an offer to take any of it on.

Letters are public: post yours on ResearchHub under this call, where others can read it and build on it. There is no
deadline; letters are read as they arrive.

### How letters are weighed

- **Usefulness now.** What the study would settle, and how much of the rest depends on it.
- **Cost-effectiveness.** What it would settle for what it would cost. Small, decisive studies are as welcome as large
  ones, and a low price alone is not enough.
- **Experience and capability.** Whether the people, the instruments and the access to participants are in place to do
  it well and without delay.

### What happens next

Every letter will be read, and promising ones followed up with questions. A small study may be funded on its letter
and a conversation. Where a full proposal is needed, it will be asked for, and the time it takes to write will be paid
for, whether or not the study is then funded.

It is hoped that funded work will register its plan in public before data are collected, report whatever the result,
share its data and code, and keep a simple public log of updates: frequent one-liners are ideal ("first three
participants recorded", "switched from thermal imaging to laser speckle"). These weigh in selection.

No cost ceiling is set.

### Who may apply, and how awards are made

Researchers and clinicians at accredited universities, colleges and academically affiliated research hospitals may
apply. A principal investigator there takes institutional responsibility for the work; student-led projects are
welcome under qualified supervision. Each award is made as a charitable gift to the recipient institution, so it
carries the institution's gift fee and not a sponsored-project indirect cost rate. Awards assume access to existing
equipment, facilities and qualified supervision; major equipment purchases are unlikely to fit.

### The studies

Fourteen ideas, grouped by what each needs and listed in the order they build on one another, not by importance. They
are possibilities, assessed only quickly, and some may not be worth doing: your own assessment counts for more. After
each title is the kind of study it would be. The Research page sets each out in full, with what it might show and what
it likely needs: https://ejhong.github.io/knots/research/#studies. The twelve on knots are listed here; the two on
runaway release follow in the next section.

Throughout, a knot means the kind described above, known by a release its owner feels. A study that marks or counts
knots should say how it would know that they are of this kind; a bump or a tender spot is not enough. People who
foam-roll regularly are a likely group to begin with: many may know this release in themselves, and they can be
studied doing only what they already do. Bodyworkers, who feel knots in others, are another.

**First: finding knots**

1. What people who foam-roll feel. *Survey and phenomenological interviews.* https://ejhong.github.io/knots/research/#study-1
2. Whether another hand can feel a knot. *Blinded reliability study (test–retest and inter-rater).* https://ejhong.github.io/knots/research/#study-2
3. What an instrument finds where a knot is felt. *Blinded comparison: felt knots against matched spots, before and after release.* https://ejhong.github.io/knots/research/#study-3

**Then: with knots found**

4. Catch a knot as it lets go. *Sham-controlled laboratory physiology, with felt releases marked as they happen.* https://ejhong.github.io/knots/research/#study-4
5. Where knots sit. *Blinded mapping, with spatial statistics against chance.* https://ejhong.github.io/knots/research/#study-5
6. Whether a knot is a stable thing. *Longitudinal: blinded mapping repeated over weeks and months.* https://ejhong.github.io/knots/research/#study-6
7. Whether knots grow with age. *Epidemiology: cross-sectional across ages, then a cohort followed over years.* https://ejhong.github.io/knots/research/#study-7
8. Whether animals have knots. *Comparative (veterinary): a survey, examination by instrument, and opportunistic necropsy.* https://ejhong.github.io/knots/research/#study-8

**At any time**

9. What is already recorded. *Secondary analysis of existing recordings.* https://ejhong.github.io/knots/research/#study-9
10. What cadavers show. *Anatomy and histology: existing dissection and post-mortem records, then dissection where knots are commonly reported.* https://ejhong.github.io/knots/research/#study-10
11. Acupoints on perforators. *Blinded mapping against chance, or a retrospective study of CT angiograms.* https://ejhong.github.io/knots/research/#study-11
12. Whether vasomotion stops in deep meditation. *Laboratory physiology with first-person event marking: fNIRS or fMRI, and laser Doppler flowmetry.* https://ejhong.github.io/knots/research/#study-12

Or one not listed: anything that might help tell what a knot is.

### Runaway release: a second question

Two informal reports describe a rarer process that can follow forceful, long work at clearing knots, in contemplative
practice or in somatic work. Past some point, releases come in chains and the process can no longer be set down; it
runs on over months or years. It is arduous and can be dangerous, above all for someone facing it alone (the project's
Research page: https://ejhong.github.io/knots/research/). This is why the studies here ask no one to press, breathe or work harder or longer than they
usually do (Care, below).

Anyone struggling with it should seek medical advice; Cheetah House (https://www.cheetahhouse.org), which supports
meditators in distress, is one further resource. Anyone thinking about suicide should reach out now: in the US, call or
text 988; elsewhere, find a line at https://findahelpline.com.

No study of it as such is known, so describing it comes first, and that could begin now. One or two possibly might be
introduced, with their agreement, once a letter is taken up. So this branch is conditional on their agreement. Once a
profile is determined, others might be found in what is already recorded, and through teachers, clinicians and support
services. It may be recorded under other names: studies of meditation-related difficulty describe currents of energy
and involuntary movement (Lindahl 2017) and energy-like somatic experiences (Cooper 2021), and researchers with that
experience are best placed. Examining people in it is limited for now to those one or two, who may define the profile,
if they are willing: no one is recruited for it, and a laboratory already measuring knots (studies 3 and 4) could add
it.

13. Characterise runaway release. *Phenomenology and natural history: a search of what is already recorded, semi-structured interviews and a case series.* https://ejhong.github.io/knots/research/#study-13
14. Examine someone in runaway release. *Observational: case studies with repeated imaging, then a prospective case series.* https://ejhong.github.io/knots/research/#study-14

### Care

Any study with people needs ethics review, comfortable pressure and a plan for referral, and work with animals or with
human tissue needs the approvals its institution requires, all before work begins. Breathing should be ordinary or,
for people who already use a breath to release their knots, that breath as they already use it; no one should be
taught a breath, or asked to press, breathe or work harder or longer, or to release more knots, than they usually do.
Nothing here is a practice or a protocol.

No one should be asked to begin or seek runaway release. People interviewed about it should be heard by researchers
who know meditation-related difficulty, with support at hand, and should not be identifiable in anything published.
What came before it matters for knowing who is at risk: it should be reported for that purpose, and not in a form that
could serve as a method.
